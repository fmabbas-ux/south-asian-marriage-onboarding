# 02. The Dynamics

For each dynamic: **what it looks like**, **why it happens**, **what each partner feels**, **what to do**, and **early warning signs**. "She" and "he" are used for the common pattern (patrilocal household, bride moves in), but every item has a reversed version (ghar damad, wife earns more, couple lives separately) covered in `05-scenario-library.md`.

---

## D1. The couple themselves

### Looks like
- Two near-strangers sharing a room, bathroom, bed and schedule from night one.
- Habits clash: sleep time, cleanliness, phone use, food, temperature, noise, guests, prayer/religious practice, social media, spending.
- Different silent rules about conflict: one raises voice, one goes quiet; one needs to talk now, one needs space.
- Each assumes the other "knows" what a husband/wife does, based on the home they grew up in.

### Why
Marriage roles are inherited, not negotiated. Each partner carries a script from their own parents' marriage. Love-marriage couples have it too: dating is not cohabiting.

### Feels
- She: loss of home, routines and autonomy; being evaluated; fear of saying the wrong thing.
- He: pressure to be the stable one; confusion at her sadness; awkwardness about intimacy and about being "caught" between two worlds.

### Do
- **Expectations audit before the wedding** (see course Module 2): who cooks, who earns, who decides, where we live, how often we visit each family, how we spend, children timing, religion in daily life, career moves.
- Agree a **conflict protocol**: soft start, 20-minute break rule, no contempt, repair phrases.
- **Weekly 30-minute check-in** ("What went well, what was hard, what I need this week").
- Intimacy: separate the physical from the emotional, go slowly, talk before the wedding night, no performance pressure, seek medical advice for pain or anxiety without shame.
- Build a **couple culture** (small rituals) so you are a unit before the family absorbs you.

### Warning signs
Silent treatment lasting days, sarcasm and eye-rolling (contempt), one partner always "the adjuster", sleeping in separate rooms without agreement, secrets about money.

## D2. Each partner's parents

### Looks like
**Her parents:** over-calling, advising her to "keep quiet and adjust", or the reverse, telling her to stand up; visiting too much or gatekeeping; comparing her husband's family with her own; interfering in money (jahez, gifts, support for them); expecting her to care for them financially or physically.

**His parents:** treating the bride as staff or as a rival; rules on dress, outings, visits to her parents, job, diet; expecting the son's salary to go to the household; mother measuring her son's attention; father silent or authoritarian; "log kya kahenge" as the final argument.

### Why
Parents have invested decades and fear loss of status, loss of the son, or loss of the daughter's safety. The mother's identity and security often rest on her son. Many mothers-in-law were treated the same way once, and recycle it.

### Feels
- Parents: threatened, unseen, anxious about reputation.
- Couple: guilt, split loyalty, resentment, dread of phone calls.

### Do
- **Pre-marriage:** the couple agrees on a **united front** before conversations with either set: "we decide together, then each speaks to their own parents."
- **Rule of own parent:** the son speaks to his parents about difficult things; the daughter speaks to hers. Never make the spouse the messenger or the villain.
- **Honour, don't obey:** respect and visits are given freely; decisions about the couple's life are the couple's. Use warm language: "Ammi, we have decided. I want your dua."
- **Translate for each other:** "She is not rejecting you; she's overwhelmed." Do not carry insults across.
- Give parents a **role** (a standing Friday dinner, a decision they are consulted on) so they feel included, not displaced.
- Money: agree beforehand what each set of parents will and won't receive, in what amount, and who communicates it.

### Warning signs
Husband never contradicts his mother, even privately; wife's parents arrive uninvited and stay; one partner hears criticism of their family daily; the couple has no private decisions.

## D3. Siblings

### Looks like
- **Sister-in-law (nand):** controls information, tests the bride, competes for the brother's attention, reports to mother, or becomes a real friend.
- **Brother-in-law / bhabhi:** another set of opinions on money, inheritance, who "deserves" what.
- **Own siblings:** relying on the married sibling financially, expecting priority, or the couple's own sibling criticising the spouse.
- **Unmarried sibling's rishta pressure:** "your marriage must not delay hers."
- Sibling politics in the joint family: share of house, shop, land, parents' care.

### Why
Siblings are also adjusting: the household is renegotiating place and attention. Inheritance and care duties create real material competition.

### Do
- Treat siblings as **individuals**, not as a bloc. Build one-to-one relationships early.
- **Do not confide** marital complaints to any sibling on either side. One sentence can reach the parents.
- Clarify **financial support to siblings** as a couple decision, with a number and a limit.
- When a nand or devar causes friction, the spouse of the family member addresses it first, privately, "tum bhi mere liye important ho, par ye cheez theek nahi."
- Inheritance and house share questions: raise them early and neutrally, in writing if possible.

### Warning signs
Information about your private life appears in the family WhatsApp group; money flows to a sibling without the spouse knowing; the spouse's siblings are always right.

## D4. Extended family: aunts, uncles, cousins, the community

### Looks like
- Comments on weight, complexion, cooking, dowry, clothing, "when is the good news?"
- Comparison with cousins; public criticism at gatherings; unsolicited marriage advice.
- Obligations: attending every event, gifts, hosting, rasm-o-riwaj.
- Family hierarchies: a dominant phupho, taya or khala whose opinion moves the elders.
- Gossip networks that turn one private incident into a family-wide story.

### Why
In collective cultures, marriage is a family and community event, so it is also a family and community audit.

### Do
- Prepare **three standard answers** for the three standard questions ("When are you having a baby?", "Why aren't you working / why are you working?", "Where will you live?"), delivered together, pleasantly, and with a smile that closes the topic.
- **One front door:** the couple does not argue in front of relatives; disagreements are settled in private.
- Pick your **events**: attend the essential ones (weddings, deaths, major Eids) and negotiate the rest.
- Identify the **influencers** of your family and build respect with them, since they shape the narrative.
- Do not use extended-family members as mediators unless both partners agree on the person.

### Warning signs
A relative is the first to know before the spouse; every gathering ends in a fight in the car; one partner hides what was said to them.

## D5. Ambitions: career, study, money, migration

### Looks like
- A working wife whose in-laws expected her to stay home, or a stay-at-home wife whose own parents expected her to work.
- A husband whose family expects every rupee, or a husband whose ambitions mean relocation.
- Higher education or professional exams, job offers abroad, business risk, a promotion that requires travel.
- Unequal earning: wife earns more; husband unemployed or underemployed; financial dependence.
- Disagreement on risk, savings, loans, property.

### Why
Gender roles are changing faster than household expectations. Money exposes unspoken assumptions about status, security and control.

### Do
- Write a **five-year picture** each, then merge: career, location, children, house, support to parents.
- Agree on **money structure**: joint account, individual accounts, household contribution, emergency fund, support to each side's parents, and a monthly money meeting.
- **Career rule:** nobody's career is switched off by default. If one must step back, decide jointly, time-boxed, with a return path.
- Handle relocation as a **joint decision with a review date**, not a unilateral announcement.
- Keep **financial transparency**: both know income, debts, loans, family obligations before the nikah.

### Warning signs
One partner learns of a loan or a family obligation after the wedding; one partner's money is "mine" and the other's is "ours"; ambition is described as selfishness.

## D6. External sources: previous marriages, children, exes, friends, social media

### Looks like
- **Previous marriage and children:** step-children, custody and visitation, the former spouse's role, financial obligations, the first family's relatives.
- A **second wife** situation: legal and emotional complexity, existing wife and children, family politics.
- **Widow/widower remarriage:** the late spouse's family, the children's acceptance, the community's views.
- **Exes** and past relationships: unresolved contact, social media, old friends.
- **Friends and colleagues:** advice from the unhappily married or the single; a work-spouse dynamic.
- **Social media:** comparison with other couples, posting the marriage, WhatsApp groups, privacy of arguments.
- **Matchmakers, religious figures, and "well-wishers"** who insert themselves.

### Do
- **Full disclosure before the nikah.** Previous marriage(s), children, debts, health conditions that affect the marriage (disclosed in a way that respects privacy), legal obligations, and family dependants.
- Step-parenting: go slowly, be an **additional trusted adult** and not a replacement parent, defer discipline to the biological parent at first, protect the children's relationship with the other parent, and make financial arrangements explicit.
- Agree **contact rules** with exes (none, limited, with the spouse's knowledge), and post nothing you would not want a relative to read.
- Choose **one or two trusted confidants** outside the family (a counsellor, a mature friend) rather than public advice.

### Warning signs
A partner hides contact with an ex or a former spouse; one partner is asked to compete with a "first family"; step-children are used as leverage by adults.

## D7. Open slot: other sources, defined later

This section is where new dynamics are added. Each entry should use the same template (looks like / why / feels / do / warning signs) and get a contributor name and review date.

**Candidates already identified:**
- Health and disability (one partner, a child, or a parent)
- Fertility, delayed pregnancy, miscarriage, gender preference of children
- In-law living arrangement changes (property, renovations, a parent's illness)
- Religion practice differences (sect, level of observance, in-laws' religious expectations)
- Cross-sect, cross-caste, cross-language and cross-ethnic marriages
- Diaspora: acculturation gap, visa dependence, remote parents, remittances
- Domestic workers and household staff politics
- Debt and family business entanglement
- Political, ideological and social-media views
- Mental health: depression, anxiety, trauma history
- Addiction, gambling and compulsive behaviours
- Violence, abuse and coercive control (see `10-red-lines-and-safety.md`; this is not a "dynamic to manage")
